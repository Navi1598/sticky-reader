However, a century of fire suppression has allowed dry vegetation to accumulate in forests that historically had frequently burned.

Rising temperatures caused by human activities such as burning fossil fuels have increased the rate at which the atmosphere pulls moisture from the land, drying soils and vegetation. Researchers estimate that this drying nearly doubled the area burned in western U.S. forests between 1984 and 2015 compared with what would have burned without human-caused climate change.

More people also now live in and at the edges of wildland areas , raising the risk of fires reaching neighborhoods. Human activity has extended the fire season beyond summer, when most lightning-caused fires occur, too. Power lines, vehicles, machinery, burning of debris and campfires can spark fires year-round.

These interacting factors have helped nearly quadruple the annual area burned in the western United States since 2000.
